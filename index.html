Research plan

Three years of building recognizers for 8 kHz calls, radio, and noise taught me how much a channel changes speech. Fraud with synthetic voices happens over the phone. In a PhD, I want to study how audio deepfake detectors behave on that kind of speech, with three questions.

  1. Does the channel fool the detector?

    Pass the same genuine and synthetic utterances through telephone and radio channels, fix the decision threshold, and count the two errors separately: genuine speech flagged as fake, and fakes that get through.

  2. What about generators it has never seen?

    Break missed detections down by generator family, instead of reporting one pooled error rate that hides which systems get through.

  3. Does it hold up in Korean?

    Korean speech over Korean phone networks, against Korean-language voice synthesis, as one concrete non-English case where the detector's training data and the real calls differ.

What I bring to it

  • Production ASR for 8 kHz calls and radio, including an 11,000-hour training set I rebuilt from undocumented legacy data.
  • About 4,180 hours of TTS training speech that I generated and augmented for three domain-adaptation projects.
  • A habit of reporting what each gain costs: general-domain regressions, and closed-set versus open-set gaps.

Telephone and radio channels (8 kHz)

2024–2026

2026 Radio and emergency-call ASR+19.21 pp on emergency-network speech and +6.74 pp on radio, for a 0.06 pp cost on general speech.
Problem
Years of domain training data had been handed over with no record of what was in it, and the base model fell apart on radio and emergency-network speech: 50.15% accuracy on the newest test set.
Approach
Rebuilt an 11,000-hour training set from the undocumented legacy data: emergency calls, spoken numbers, TTS-generated domain abbreviations, and emergency-network speech, including training data I recovered after it had gone missing. Upsampled the 8 kHz audio to 16 kHz, trained four candidate models, and chose an average of the five best checkpoints.
Result
Emergency network 72.34% → 91.55% (+19.21 pp). Radio +6.74 pp on average, and +38.93 pp on the newest test set. General speech 91.02% → 90.96% (−0.06 pp).
2024–26 Government call-center ASR+0.87 pp to about 94% on real calls, against a general-speech regression of about 1 pp.
Problem
Adapt the 8 kHz model to a government call center with 50 hours of its real calls.
Approach
Adapted with the real calls plus call-noise augmentation. Before choosing a model, traced the general-domain regression across the base, dialect, and noise test sets.
Result
+0.87 pp, to about 94% domain accuracy.
Caveat
The gain is about the same size as the general-domain cost, roughly 1 pp.
2026 One voice activity detector for 8 and 16 kHzTwo models merged into one: 93.30% average accuracy, +3.43 pp at 8 kHz. Deployed April 2026.
Problem
Telephone (8 kHz) and wideband (16 kHz) audio each had their own always-on voice activity model, and merging them opened a performance gap between the two domains.
Approach
Fifteen rounds of experiments: a hyperparameter search, then boundary labels refined with a speaker-diarization model, a noise-filtering criterion based on how many syllables the recognizer finds in a segment, data and learning-rate changes, and noise augmentation. I chose the model with the smallest gap between the two domains, not the best single score. Benchmarked against Silero VAD, TEN VAD, and FireRed VAD with start-point, end-point, and false-positive error analysis.
Result

8 kHz base91.82%95.25%+3.43 pp

The gains are small on 16 kHz audio. Error analysis of start points and false positives set the direction for the next version.

Problem
A new domain of maintenance commands and technical terms, with no real recordings and constant engine and airframe noise.
Approach
Wrote about 25,000 command scripts, generated about 3,000 hours of TTS speech with eight voices and varied pitch and tempo, and mixed in helicopter, engine, and wind noise at 20 dB SNR. Adapted on top of a 10,734-hour base set and averaged the checkpoints from epochs 17 and 18.

2024 Voice commands for delivery ridersAbout 280 hours of TTS: +1.75 pp on the domain and +4.79 pp overall.
Problem
Riders accept and sort delivery calls by voice while riding. The base model missed service-specific commands such as accepting the nearest call, and real rider recordings were hard to get.
Approach
Adapted with about 280 hours of generated speech on top of an 11,958-hour base, and validated on test sets recorded with five devices. After deployment, new prompts were misrecognized, so I generated 882 more utterances (7 prompts, 7 voices, augmented), re-adapted on the new base model, and designed a new test set recorded by 5 speakers on 4 devices.

+1.75 pp across the five device test sets, +14.90 pp on the new test sets, and +4.79 pp overall. The first model shipped, and the post-deployment fixes went through the same loop.
2024 Child articulation testsAbout 10 hours of target-domain child speech, over 92% agreement.
Problem
The target domain had about 10 hours of recordings, 0.8% of the training data. The first model scored 99.60% on general child speech but 67.09% on the target domain.
Approach
Trained an end-to-end model from scratch with 57 Korean phoneme outputs (KoG2P) on 1,258 hours of child speech, then added augmentation, TTS voices, and generated data in stages over 17 experiments. Rebuilt transcripts after clinical review and analyzed errors phoneme by phoneme.
Result
Over 92% agreement: 92.25% on recorder audio and 92.82% on phone audio. Training on the domain data alone had reached 73.97%.
Caveat
The final model was scored separately on recorder and phone audio, while the early results pooled them, so the comparison with 67.09% is not exact.

Data quality and tooling

2023–2025

2023–24 Cleaning training data without losing accentsOver 3,000 hours cleaned with a two-part filter that removes mistranscriptions but keeps accented speech.
Problem
Public training corpora contained mistranscribed utterances. A plain CER threshold would also discard valid speech that is simply accented, and decoding all 2,169 hours of dialect data just to screen it was inefficient.
Approach
My hypothesis was that mistranscribed utterances produce more insertions than substitutions, so the filter required both a CER above 15% and more insertions than substitutions. I listened to every high-error utterance to build a 10-type error taxonomy. For dialect data, I used a filler-word heuristic and set questionable utterances aside instead of deleting them.
Result
Over 3,000 hours cleaned. Of the errors I listened to, 43.2% were poor pronunciation, 30.4% low volume, and 26.4% noise, extra speech, and other types. Extended in 2024 to five dialects, loanwords, spontaneous speech, and Korean–English code-switching.
Caveat
The filter's effect on downstream CER was not measured in a matched training experiment.
2025 Turning subtitled drama into training dataAn SRT and WAV to Hugging Face datasets pipeline that became the team's standard.
Problem
Subtitles mix the spoken words with text that does not match the audio: tags, credits, song lyrics, and lines in other languages.
Approach
Parsed SRT and WAV pairs into Hugging Face datasets as an intermediate format, then converted them to the team's training format. Wrote filtering rules for each kind of mismatch and sent the hard cases to manual listening.
Result
Subtitle contentWhy it failsAction
A tag such as [Music]No speech at allRemoved by rule
Song lyricsSung, not spokenRemoved by rule
A line in another languageNot Korean speechRemoved by rule
Korean subtitle over foreign-language audioText and audio disagreeManual listening

The pipeline is documented with a flow chart and is now the team's standard corpus recipe.

Graduate work

Graduate Researcher, Auditory Intelligence Lab, Sogang University, 2021 to 2023, advised by Prof. Ji-Hwan Kim.

Master's thesis: grammar WFST fusion for Korean end-to-end ASR

CTC models predict each output token independently, so they learn no grammar from their transcripts. I compiled token, lexicon, and grammar transducers into one search graph with NeMo and k2, and used it to decode two off-the-shelf Korean CTC models from NVIDIA, Citrinet-1024 and Conformer-L, without retraining them. Korean adds a twist: some real syllables are missing from the models' output vocabularies, so the lexicon maps each one to a similar-sounding token, such as 륄 in 브륄레 (brûlée) to 릴.

The pattern follows perplexity. Where the grammar had seen sentences much like the test set, CER fell by 75 to 87%. On conversation, the same grammar forced the wrong words, and CER rose by 46 to 59%. Shallow fusion helped on every test set, though on read speech it gained less than the grammar did. A token-level grammar, built on the models' subword units instead of words, raised CER to 31 to 57% on read news and conversation.

Caveat The grammar was trained on each corpus's own training text. A perplexity of 2.08 on Zeroth Korean means its test sentences were highly predictable from that text, so 0.66% is an in-domain result, not an open-vocabulary one. The thesis reports the conversational losses as the method's main limitation.

Other graduate research

2021–2023

2022–23 Measuring dysarthria severity after strokeClinical measures computed from recognizer output tracked human ratings with correlations of 0.94 to 0.97.
Problem
Dysarthria assessment relies on therapists listening and counting. Automating it first requires showing that machine measurements agree with the clinical ones. Part of a project on conversation-based, multimodal explainable AI.
Approach
Separated patient speech from therapist–patient sessions, built the database, and did the human annotation myself. Computed alternating and sequential motion rates (AMR, SMR) from repetitions of 퍼 터 커 [pʰʌ tʰʌ kʰʌ] with a phoneme Conformer (trained on 2,600 hours of elderly conversational speech, 2.20% phoneme error rate), and the percentage of correct consonants (PCC) and syllables per second (SPS) from Whisper Large transcripts of word and paragraph reading.
Result
Clinical measureRecognizerCorrelation with human rating
AMRPhoneme Conformer0.94
SMRPhoneme Conformer0.95
PCCWhisper Large0.94
SPSWhisper Large0.97

A severity-stage classifier on these features reached 74.85% accuracy, against 60.73% for a random forest and 53.37% for XGBoost.

Caveat
Raw error rates (PER, CER) did not track severity well; the clinical measures did. The data skewed toward men with mild impairment, which I flagged for the project's second year, along with adding sex, age, and intelligibility as features.

2021–22 Voice commands for unmanned vehiclesA WFST recognizer on the controller: 2.38% WER on a 36-word command grammar.
Problem
Operators of unmanned aerial, surface, and ground vehicles should be able to give commands by voice, and a misrecognized command is a safety problem.
Approach
Composed a LibriSpeech-pretrained DNN-HMM acoustic model, a 4-gram language model covering every sentence of the command grammar (vehicle, action, speed, distance or heading, then “execute”), and a G2P lexicon into one search graph.
Result
2.38% WER on command-grammar utterances, presented as first author at KSAS 2021. I also co-authored the Whisper-based follow-up.
Caveat
Measured on in-grammar speech only. Rejecting out-of-grammar speech and response time need their own tests.
